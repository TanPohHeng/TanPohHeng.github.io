Megaman Maker Map

Written by

Poh Heng

in

Design, Projects

I designed these 3 stages in MegaMan Maker for a design module project. The levels are available to play by downloading the MegaMan Maker client and the save files.

My Design Process

For this section, I’ll focus on this stage

The levels I made for this project generally start with a focus on the mechanics. In general, the theme of the stage follows the theme of the mechanics used in the puzzles of the stage. This restriction helps with creating a cohesive visual identity for the stage.

Mechanics

When it comes to choosing mechanics for a stage, I try to look for mechanics that have interactions with other mechanics or would otherwise fit thematically to the main mechanic. The basic interaction is usually tested in a sandbox area in the map where these interactions eventually become the foundation of the puzzles that are used in the level.

For this particular stage, I ended up choosing the main mechanic to be water, or rather the water height in a level. Other mechanics I ended up using as compliment were:

  • Acid Puddle
  • Water Level switch
  • Prawn or Whale enemies (different states when in water)
  • Waterfall
  • Thunder weapon
  • Acid Dropper

These are the interactions I wanted to use to build my levels. You can find videos of interactions here.

Designing the level

For this level, I specifically wanted less combat and more puzzle solving. As such, double damage was enabled and the weapons equipped by Proto Man either did not do damage, or consumed resources to damage enemies. This was done in the hope to get players to approach the level with a more puzzle focused mindset instead of blindly charging ahead like in more combat oriented levels.

In order to introduce the puzzle mechanics to the player, I tried to introduce each mechanic in this order:

  • Create a safe space for the player to learn the mechanic
  • Introduce one area where the player can interact with the mechanic
  • At least one puzzle where the interaction is the key to progress

The main idea in theory was to slowly introduce the notable puzzle mechanics to the players to ensure that they would have the key ideas needed to complete the level.

Often how each puzzle was created on the idea of a specific interaction between 2 or more mechanics. Below, you can see the note that I wrote for planning each of the puzzles in the level.

On the left is a list of the intended challenge mechanics and on the right are the mechanics used in each obstacle faced by the player. The letters on the right each represent a mechanic on the left (i.e. Acid puddle = A, Water level switch = B, etc.) and each ” ‘ ” symbol meant an inversion of how the mechanic was used was an element in the obstacle. This helped me track the cadence of the obstacles presented to the player in the level, so that I could tune the difficulty of the level to adjust the player experience.

Level visual design

Mega man levels are made of grid based wall/ground blocks that are fairly interchangeable, which makes initial blocking of a level easier as the tile set needn’t be decided at the iteration stage. Once the level’s core puzzles and challenges have been designed, I start trying out different tile sets to get a feel for the ascetics.

I would often test multiple different tile designs and backgrounds to work towards a theme for the stage.

Most tiles in this level are chosen to fit a theme of an undersea infiltration into a floating city. This is why the level design changes tile sets across the different frames in the level

Feedback & Reflections

As the level was done for a university module, I was lucky enough to receive direct feedback for each level. To break down the feedback given for the level, they mainly come down to these 3 points:

  • visual information presentation needs work
  • gameplay concept consistency
  • too many mechanics

An example of visual information presentation is in this frame.

Upon entering this frame, the player naturally has their eyes drawn to the 2 moving objects on the screen: the whale enemy moving to the left and Proto Man controlled by the player moving right. The 2 moving elements are moving in the opposite direction help lure the attention of the players to the lower half of the frame since that is where all the movement occurs.

This however reduces attention to the top part of the screen where 2 other mechanics are in play:

  • Light green circle: Acid puddles are neutralized by water up to the current water level
  • Red circle: A water level switch that is important to an encounter in the next frame.

The water level switch in the red circle lowers the water level to make a prawn in the other connected frame run away from the player and not cause an unavoidable damage interaction. Additionally, without activating the switch, the player does not learn that whale enemies self destruct when not in water.

Without learning this interaction, the player simply takes damage on entering the next frame. This would change the information the player would know during the course of the level.

Given the importance of the water level switch, I think a better position for it might have been something like this:

This would place the switch naturally in the expected line of motion of the player and made it naturally emphasized by the player character’s movement to the right. This should get more players to naturally interact with the switch by using the movement of the character to direct the player’s attention to the new water switch position.

On further reflection, part of the reason why the visuals of the level are so crowded in some parts is due to the number of mechanics I attempt to place in a single obstacle.

In this particular frame alone, there is 4 mechanics (waterfall, acid water, acid drops and the water level under the acid that is safe to stay in) passively at play in a cycle to create a room where the acid is safe to traverse only during certain parts of the cycle. The fact that the acid changes color so rapidly due to the waterfall neutralizing the acid while the acid drops changes a water layer back into acid does not help the visual clarity of the level.

Although this combination of mechanics help create a timing challenge for the player, having these many interactions happening constantly makes for poor visual clarity. In addition, the waterfall and acid base neutralization interaction is only introduced this frame, so it makes the objectives I intended the player to learn rather loaded as they need to solve a puzzle, learn 2 new mechanical interaction, get a key and collect a new weapon all in a single room. It is probably a better solution to spit the interactions in this room to 2 or 3 separate frames to better allow the player the space to understand the interactions happening before them. Either that, or reduce the total number of interactions in the room to help it’s readability by the player.

Further Reflections

Now as I look back and reevaluate the level after some time has passed, there are 2 more points I think my level could have provided a better experience for:

  • Better layout to communicate design intent
  • And more allowance in the strictness for some obstacles

What I mean for a better layout is how I think some of the later frames like the one in the previous section could have re-arranged some of the key interactable to better guide the players in the right direction, or to teach them important interactions.

Because some obstacles in the level have very little leeway for error in some stages(like falling off the map due to a waterfall, or the strict timing for acid drops contaminating water when neutralized), the level’s stake feels more “all or nothing” on some obstacles rather than in others. While many of these scenarios can feel rather stressful to play through, it is likely adjustments required to make the experience less stressful will not be a one size suits all solution. This means it is likely that I will need to be more aware of the challenge each frame provides for the player in order to better tune the playing experience.
